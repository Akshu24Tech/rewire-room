# Sahar Yousef: Stories

Personal experiments, laboratory demonstrations, and consulting case studies used by Dr. Sahar Yousef to illustrate cognitive neuroscience principles.

## The Multitasking Interleaved Writing Experiment

In live lectures and corporate workshops, Dr. Yousef frequently demonstrates the switch cost using a simple paper-and-pen test. Participants are instructed to draw two horizontal lines. In trial one, they write the phrase "I am a great multitasker" on the top line, then sequentially write the numbers 1 through 20 on the bottom line. In trial two, they interleave the letters and numbers: "I", "1", " ", "2", "a", "3", "m", "4", and so on. Invariably, trial two takes more than double the time, induces visible stress, and generates multiple calculation errors. This visceral demonstration proves that the human brain does not possess parallel execution capabilities, vividly illustrating how rapid task switching burns mental stamina (S001).

## The Silent Phone in the Room Demonstration

Dr. Yousef cites rigorous cognitive experiments investigating the mere presence of mobile devices. In these trials, participants complete demanding working memory and fluid intelligence tests under three conditions: phone on the desk, phone in a pocket or bag, and phone in an entirely separate room. Even when phones were completely silenced, powered off, and placed face down, participants with phones on their desks scored significantly lower than those whose phones were in another room. The brain was subconsciously devoting finite executive resources to inhibit the impulse to check the device. Moving the phone physically out of sight instantly recovered measurable cognitive capacity (S002, S010).

## Re-architecting Knowledge Worker Focus at UC Berkeley

At UC Berkeley Haas School of Business and in high-growth technology firms, Dr. Yousef observed that knowledge workers were spending upwards of sixty percent of their days reacting to messages, attending fragmented 30-minute meetings, and attempting to do real work in late evening hours. By instituting synchronized focus sprints and team-wide "Focus Fridays", organizations saw dramatic increases in output and precipitous drops in self-reported exhaustion. Structuring collective cognitive boundaries allowed employees to complete their core work during standard business hours without bleeding energy into their personal lives (S003, S006).

## The Executive Afternoon Crash Transformation

In her executive coaching labs, Dr. Yousef encountered senior leaders who experienced severe mental fog, memory lapses, and irritable reactions during critical late-afternoon board meetings. Rather than diagnosing an emotional or psychological deficiency, Dr. Yousef audited their daily calendar and identified that they attended seven continuous meetings without a single biological break, while glancing at their phone between remarks. By enforcing a mandatory 15-minute zero-input walking break between morning and afternoon sessions, the executives completely eliminated their late-day brain freeze and regained steady presence (S006, S009).
## The Berkeley Haas MBA Biological Prime Time Audit

In her cognitive neuroscience courses at UC Berkeley Haas, Dr. Yousef assigned executive MBA students to systematically map their cognitive highs and lows across a two-week period. Many students who believed they were "night owls" discovered through objective performance logging that their sharpest analytical capacity occurred between 8:00 AM and 10:30 AM, while their late-night study hours were plagued by sluggish comprehension and heavy distraction. By shifting their hardest problem sets into their newly identified biological prime times, the students reported completing assignments in half the time with dramatically higher retention and reduced stress (S002, S005).
