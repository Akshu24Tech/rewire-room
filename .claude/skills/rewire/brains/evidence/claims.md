# The Evidence seat: claims

Each entry grades a claim the way a seat would say it, using only the 31 sources in `sources.md`.

How to read the grades:

- **STRONG:** several good human studies or a solid meta-analysis, consistent, surviving bias correction, in people like the user. Nothing here meets that bar yet. The liking gap comes closest.
- **MIXED:** real but limited evidence. Where an entry says "not settled by these sources", MIXED means no direct test exists here. It does not mean half proven, and it does not mean false.
- **WEAK:** thin, indirect, or borrowed from another population, or the nearest tests point the other way.
- **UNSUPPORTED:** no source here backs the claim as stated, or the direct test came back null. It means not shown. It does not mean disproven.

"Reported by E0xx" means that source describes another study that is not in this corpus.

## A few minutes of slow breathing practice each day lowers everyday stress

- **Grade:** MIXED
- **What the research shows:** Multi-session programs in everyday life and clinics lowered self-reported stress, g = -0.35, CI -0.55 to -0.14, 12 RCTs, 785 adults, with no trial at low risk of bias and no follow-up analysis (E009). In a vote count, 54 of 72 interventions reduced stress or anxiety, but with no effect sizes and poor study quality (E034).
- **Where it overreaches:** Breathwork treats anxiety or depression conditions: WEAK. The authors call recommending it premature in clinical groups (E009), and this seat recommends no treatment.
- **Key line:** "our results should be interpreted cautiously" [E009]
- **Sources:** E009, E034

## A few minutes of breathing right before a hard moment will calm you down

- **Grade:** WEAK
- **What the research shows:** Simulated-stress studies (shock anticipation, public speaking, all one-day) were the least successful group, 5 of 11. Single sessions worked in 10 of 23, and 9 of those 10 were human-guided. Sessions under 5 minutes failed in six of nine (E034). These are vote counts with no effect sizes. E009 and E033 did not test use before a stressful event.
- **Where it overreaches:** Breathing reliably settles your nerves before any talk: UNSUPPORTED. The one public speaking trial listed showed only a non-significant trend (E034).
- **Key line:** "Due to the mediocre-at-best methodological quality of included studies, some effectiveness findings may not be as reliable or valid as assumed." [E034]
- **Sources:** E034, E009, E033

## One physiological sigh in the moment drops your heart rate and calms you fast

- **Grade:** WEAK
- **What the research shows:** No source tests a single breath or a single sigh under stress. The only sighing trial used 5 minutes a day for 28 days during calm practice, did not use the term physiological sigh, and found no change in resting heart rate, measured during sleep (E033). The nearest evidence on short doses points away from the claim (E034). That is indirect, not proof a sigh does nothing.
- **Where it overreaches:** The sigh works instantly for everyone, even mid-panic: UNSUPPORTED.
- **Key line:** "This study was originally intended as an exploratory study in preparation for a larger clinical trial" [E033]
- **Sources:** E033, E034

## Cyclic sighing is the best way to breathe, and it beats mindfulness

- **Grade:** WEAK
- **What the research shows:** One exploratory remote RCT, 108 enrolled, mostly undergraduates, registered after the fact, no passive control. Cyclic sighing raised positive affect and lowered respiratory rate more than mindfulness, but its drop in state anxiety was no larger (E033). E009 did not analyze specific techniques. Andrew Huberman was senior author and narrated its videos (declared), so the Huberman seat is leaning on its own expert's study.
- **Where it overreaches:** Breathing beats meditation for anxiety: UNSUPPORTED. The direct comparison found no difference (E033).
- **Key line:** "There were no differences between the two groups in state anxiety and negative affect changes" [E033]
- **Sources:** E033, E009

## Breathing practice raises heart rate variability, tones the vagus nerve, and improves sleep

- **Grade:** UNSUPPORTED
- **What the research shows:** The one source with these outcomes found no significant change in heart rate variability, resting heart rate, or sleep in any group after 28 days of 5 minutes a day. Respiratory rate did fall, and that fall tracked mood weakly, r = -0.24 (E033). E009 and E034 had no physiological outcomes. Vagal tone was not measured. One small null does not rule out an effect.
- **Where it overreaches:** A few breaths reset your nervous system for the day: UNSUPPORTED.
- **Key line:** "Overall, breathwork was more effective than mindfulness meditation in improving positive affect, an effect that got larger with more adherence to the protocol." [E033]
- **Sources:** E033, E009, E034

## The more you practice breathing, and the better you are taught, the more it helps

- **Grade:** MIXED
- **What the research shows:** Guided first sessions, multiple sessions, and 6 or more sessions over a week were associated with effectiveness across studies (E034). Yet E009 found no dose-response across its trials (tau-b = -0.05, p = 0.418), with adherence often unreported. In E033, gains grew with days of practice only for positive affect with cyclic sighing.
- **Where it overreaches:** Any breath pattern works the same: WEAK. E034 found no association with pace once fast-only breathing was set aside, but only two interventions were fast-only, and no association is not equivalence.
- **Key line:** "The framework presented here is based on associations between breath practice components and effectiveness; it does not necessarily represent causal relationships." [E034]
- **Sources:** E034, E009, E033

## Relabel your nerves as readiness and you will perform better

- **Grade:** MIXED
- **What the research shows:** Brief reappraisal messages improved task performance, d = 0.23, CI 0.14 to 0.33, 44 effects, N = 5638. Correcting for likely publication bias cut this to d = 0.14, and the prediction interval runs from -0.18 to 0.65, so a new study could go either way (E031). Samples were mostly students in short lab and classroom tasks, none clinical. Introversion was not tested.
- **Where it overreaches:** It works especially well for public speaking: WEAK. Public tasks gave d = 0.34 against 0.20 for written tasks, but the difference was only a trend (p = 0.064), and the category includes golf putting and darts (E031).
- **Key line:** "the drop in effect size from d = 0.23 to d = 0.14 is worth noting" [E031]
- **Sources:** E031

## Reframing arousal moves your body into a challenge state and makes you feel less anxious

- **Grade:** WEAK
- **What the research shows:** One preregistered RCT of medical students in a simulated bad-news consultation found a better cardiovascular challenge pattern (beta = 0.90, p < 0.001, N = 189 with usable data), but self-rated coping did not change significantly (p = 0.087) (E032). A single lab study in another population. An earlier meta-analysis, reported by E031, found a small subjective benefit and no significant physiological effect.
- **Where it overreaches:** Reframing removes the threat response in real conversations: UNSUPPORTED. E031's outcome is performance, not feeling, and E032 ran under controlled conditions.
- **Key line:** "The SAR intervention did not significantly affect the resources-demands evaluation, thus failing to confirm hypothesis 1.1." [E032]
- **Sources:** E032, E031

## Calm the body first, because you cannot talk your mind calm

- **Grade:** MIXED
- **What the research shows:** Not settled by these sources. No study here compares a body-first tool with a mind-first tool. A brief verbal message gave a small performance gain under stress (E031), and breathing did poorly in simulated-stress settings (E034), but these are different outcomes in different studies, so neither decides the order.
- **Where it overreaches:** Mental tools fail once arousal is high: UNSUPPORTED. E031 sits awkwardly with it.
- **Key line:** "This indicates that more evidence is necessary to confirm a true effect." [E031]
- **Sources:** E031, E034

## An if-then plan helps you follow through on what you intend

- **Grade:** MIXED
- **What the research shows:** Small and uneven. Combined mental contrasting plus if-then plans: g = 0.336, or 0.242 after bias correction (E004). Adult exercise: 0.15, CI -0.01 to 0.31, not significant (E029). Drinking: d = -0.14 weekly, null for heavy episodes, all studies at high risk of bias (E028). Children: g = 0.31, with corrections from 0.15 to 0.38 (E030), which does not transfer to adults.
- **Where it overreaches:** The effect is large and reliable: UNSUPPORTED. A larger adult figure of 0.54 is only reported by E030 from other work.
- **Key line:** "the implementation intentions strategy did not significantly increase the practice of PA in comparison with the groups that did not receive this strategy" [E029]
- **Sources:** E004, E028, E029, E030

## If I feel nervous, then I will act: if-then plans stop you freezing

- **Grade:** MIXED
- **What the research shows:** Not settled by these sources. No source on if-then plans measures social fear, freezing, or conversation, and none analyzes introversion (E004, E028, E029, E030). Plans triggered by a feeling are nearly untested: one child study in E030, two samples in E028.
- **Where it overreaches:** The plan makes the action automatic when the cue comes: WEAK. These reviews describe that as theory and measure goal outcomes, not automaticity.
- **Key line:** "the absence of a significant moderating effect cannot be taken as evidence for the absence of moderation" [E030]
- **Sources:** E004, E028, E029, E030

## Repeatedly facing the situation you fear shrinks everyday social fear

- **Grade:** MIXED
- **What the research shows:** Evidence comes from treatment-style samples. Virtual reality exposure for social anxiety disorder: g = 0.88 against waitlist, prediction interval 0.03 to 1.79 (E024). Stand-alone VR with symptomatic volunteers: SMD = -0.82, CI -1.52 to -0.13, five small high-risk trials, with non-clinical applicability called uncertain (E025). Children aged 6 to 12: g = 0.82, I2 = 85%, active ingredient unknown (E027). For self-run, real-world practice in non-clinical adults, this is not settled by these sources.
- **Where it overreaches:** Exposure will rewire fear in any ordinary introvert: UNSUPPORTED. VR is as good as real life: WEAK, since no significant difference in few trials is not equivalence (E024).
- **Key line:** "there is still uncertainty as the effect estimate is based on few studies with few participants each and a high risk of bias" [E025]
- **Sources:** E024, E025, E027

## Social fear means your brain's threat learning is broken

- **Grade:** WEAK
- **What the research shows:** In lab conditioning experiments, socially anxious people did not differ from controls in learning threat (d = -0.009) or learning safety (d = 0.047, 7 experiments) (E026). The authors say lab social stimuli may be too weak to create real fear of evaluation. Everyday life was not tested.
- **Where it overreaches:** So exposure must work, or cannot work, for you: UNSUPPORTED. This review tested no treatment.
- **Key line:** "social anxiety is not reliably related to deficits in conditioning and extinction processes in the context of laboratory-based Pavlovian conditioning paradigms" [E026]
- **Sources:** E026

## Stop hiding: avoidance makes you more anxious and less liked, so drop it

- **Grade:** MIXED
- **What the research shows:** In one lab experiment, 96 non-clinical students told to use safety behaviors in 5-minute talks felt more anxious (b = 9.08). The avoidance type also made partners like them less (b = -11.79), and both people enjoyed the talk less (E023). A one-time survey links avoidance to social anxiety in a high-anxiety group, beta = 0.36 (E003). The experiment added the behaviors. Dropping them was not tested, and neither was everyday life.
- **Where it overreaches:** Dropping safety behaviors lowers your anxiety for good: UNSUPPORTED. Safety behaviors cause lasting social anxiety: UNSUPPORTED.
- **Key line:** "both types of safety behaviours have a causal role in increasing subjective anxiety" [E023]
- **Sources:** E023, E003

## Monitoring how you come across makes you more anxious

- **Grade:** MIXED
- **What the research shows:** Told to monitor impressions, students felt more anxious, but partners' ratings, liking, and enjoyment showed no significant change (E023). With no active control, distraction may explain part of it. In a survey, impression management had no significant direct link to social anxiety (beta = 0.05), only a path through replay in the high-anxiety group (beta = 0.13) (E003).
- **Where it overreaches:** Other people can see you doing it, and it ruins the conversation: WEAK. It causes social anxiety: UNSUPPORTED.
- **Key line:** "Both safety behaviours make the person who performs them feel more anxious." [E023]
- **Sources:** E023, E003

## Replaying the moment afterward keeps your fear alive

- **Grade:** WEAK
- **What the research shows:** The only source is a cross-sectional Korean online survey, 487 adults, comparing high and low scorers with the middle left out. Post-event replay differed strongly between groups and carried part of some links in the high group (E003). One time point cannot show direction, and the authors ask for experiments.
- **Where it overreaches:** Stop replaying and your anxiety will fall: UNSUPPORTED.
- **Key line:** "impression management can never be considered a helpful strategy because social anxiety is maintained when mediated by state and trait PEP" [E003] (the authors' wording, stronger than a one-time survey can show)
- **Sources:** E003

## You can change a personality trait on purpose, with practice

- **Grade:** MIXED
- **What the research shows:** Interventions moved traits in the desired direction, d = 0.22, CI 0.005 to 0.433, 7 studies. Extraversion was d = 0.38 on only 3 effect sizes. Mostly pre-post comparisons in young Western samples, with no risk of bias assessment. Some programs backfired, and a goal alone was only weakly linked to change (E035). Trait scores rose in E012 after one week, but the authors warn this may be mood or demand.
- **Where it overreaches:** You can permanently become an extravert, or you should: UNSUPPORTED. Wanting to change is enough: WEAK.
- **Key line:** "Although the available evidence on VPC is still limited, the initial results on VPC are promising." [E035]
- **Sources:** E035, E012

## Acting more extraverted for a while lifts your mood

- **Grade:** MIXED
- **What the research shows:** Everyday life: in a one-week crossover, 131 undergraduates gained positive affect, connection, and flow, but happiness did not differ and negative affect was inconsistent. No neutral control, and both prompts promised benefits (E012). Six weeks of acting sociable lowered loneliness (2.38 to 2.06), but it had returned to 2.33 a month later (E036). Lab: a summary page reports similar mood gains, with no numbers (E013).
- **Where it overreaches:** Introverts are better off acting extraverted in general or long term: UNSUPPORTED.
- **Key line:** "Our results suggest that personality-relevant behavior can be successfully manipulated for several days by simply instructing participants to behave differently." [E012]
- **Sources:** E012, E013, E036

## Acting extraverted costs introverts: it drains them

- **Grade:** MIXED
- **What the research shows:** Not settled by these sources. Tested: one short lab session, described only on a lab web page with no numbers, found no cost for trait introverts on a Stroop task, though extraverts showed one when acting introverted (E013). In E012, baseline trait extraversion did not moderate well-being over one week, but no introvert-only result is given and fatigue and cognitive cost were not measured. Not tested: weeks of sustained acting, direct fatigue, real work or social settings. E012 reports a short-duration no-cost result from another study; that is not E012's own finding.
- **Where it overreaches:** Introverts certainly pay a price: UNSUPPORTED. Acting extraverted certainly costs introverts nothing: UNSUPPORTED.
- **Key line:** "it is possible that introverts (as well as extraverts) may experience cognitive and hedonic costs after behaving" [E012]
- **Sources:** E012, E013

## Introverts feel more authentic when they act extraverted

- **Grade:** WEAK
- **What the research shows:** Only a lab web page says this, about in-the-moment reports, with no sample size or statistics, and it notes that strongly identified introverts may differ (E013). E012 did not measure authenticity. The underlying papers are not in this corpus.
- **Where it overreaches:** Acting extraverted never feels fake to an introvert: UNSUPPORTED.
- **Key line:** "trait introverts tell us that they feel more authentic when behaving extraverted, compared to introverted, in the moment" [E013]
- **Sources:** E013, E012

## Going quieter than usual lowers your mood

- **Grade:** MIXED
- **What the research shows:** Assigned to act more introverted for a week, undergraduates lost positive affect (about -0.55 SD), connection, and flow (E012). Negative affect results differed by order, and there was no neutral control. A lab page reports the same direction (E013). This is instructed behavior in students, not chosen solitude.
- **Where it overreaches:** Quiet time or being an introvert is bad for you: UNSUPPORTED. Neither source tests chosen quiet time.
- **Key line:** "trait extraverts performed more poorly on the cognitive task if they had been asked to behave introverted" [E013]
- **Sources:** E012, E013

## Acting more extraverted improves your immune gene expression

- **Grade:** UNSUPPORTED
- **What the research shows:** The preregistered primary test in an everyday-life RCT, N = 119, was null (p = 0.705). Positive results appeared only in a secondary pathway analysis, were gone at one month, and the authors ask for replication (E036). Trait introversion was not examined.
- **Where it overreaches:** Acting extraverted makes you healthier: UNSUPPORTED. No health outcome or immune cell function was measured.
- **Key line:** "Pre-registered analyses of pre-specified CTRA indicator genes showed no significant effects." [E036]
- **Sources:** E036

## Every time you recall a memory, you rewrite it

- **Grade:** WEAK
- **What the research shows:** Seven lab experiments with 112 people found no reconsolidation effect: accuracy fell under 1% against about 57% in the original, and in the conceptual replications the no-reminder group did worse, the opposite of the prediction (E015). A replication with 96 students found only anecdotal support on the key comparison, BF10 = 1.99 (E037). The original authors, with no new data, argue boundary conditions and report many positive studies elsewhere (E014).
- **Where it overreaches:** Reconsolidation is proven in people: WEAK. Reconsolidation has been disproven in people: UNSUPPORTED, since nulls cannot prove absence (E014, E015, E037).
- **Key line:** "These findings suggest that memory retrieval followed by new learning does not reliably induce human memory updating via reconsolidation." [E015]
- **Sources:** E015, E037, E014

## Recall the embarrassing moment, add something new, and you rewrite the memory

- **Grade:** UNSUPPORTED
- **What the research shows:** No source tests embarrassing or social memories, or mental replay. The closest tests used sequences and lab pictures and did not reliably show the effect. For negative pictures the key comparison was BF10 = 1.17 (E015, E037). So this has not been shown, and it has not been shown impossible.
- **Where it overreaches:** You can erase a painful memory or trauma on your own: UNSUPPORTED. Real trauma goes to a professional.
- **Key line:** "the reconsolidation process may not be as reliable or as robust as previous reconsolidation studies have suggested." [E037]
- **Sources:** E015, E037

## Animal studies show fear memories can be wiped when reactivated

- **Grade:** WEAK
- **What the research shows:** A review of 95 drug-vehicle comparisons in 1,896 rats and mice found signs of publication bias, I2 = 75%, and about 80% of published comparisons significant against 20% of unpublished ones (E038). The authors do not claim the effect is absent. Drug-induced amnesia in rodents does not transfer to people or behavioral practice, which lowers the grade for this user.
- **Where it overreaches:** So your fear memories can be erased: UNSUPPORTED.
- **Key line:** "Only 20% of the shared unpublished experiments found a statistically significant amnestic effect" [E038]
- **Sources:** E038

## Results in cells show how your practice should be spaced

- **Grade:** WEAK
- **What the research shows:** No source in this corpus tests a cell result, or whether one carries over to people. That leaves an analogy, not a human finding, and cell evidence does not transfer to a self-image. As a nearby caution, even rodent memory results looked more robust in print than they proved (E038). That is about rodents, not cells.
- **Where it overreaches:** Spaced reps rewrite you because cells respond to spacing, and 10 minutes is the right gap: UNSUPPORTED.
- **Key line:** "Reactivation-dependent amnesia for contextual fear memories in rodents is thus less robust than what is projected by the literature." [E038]
- **Sources:** E038

## Space your practice and a skill will stick better

- **Grade:** MIXED
- **What the research shows:** For factual exam material in medical students: of 21 studies, effects were positive in 10, mixed in 5, neutral in 5, none at low risk of bias, and 12 gave no effect size. Few separate spacing from retrieval, and none compared massed cramming (E039). Another population. For social skills: not settled by these sources.
- **Where it overreaches:** Spaced reps build speaking up faster than massed reps: UNSUPPORTED. The more reps, the better: WEAK, since heavy users chose their own intensity (E039).
- **Key line:** "No included study was determined to be at low risk of bias." [E039]
- **Sources:** E039

## Spreading out small reps will change the story you tell about yourself

- **Grade:** MIXED
- **What the research shows:** Not settled by these sources. The only spacing source covers exam recall in medical students (E039). No source tests spaced social reps, self-labels, or self-story. Volitional trait change work did not test spacing (E035).
- **Where it overreaches:** Spacing is shown to rewrite who you think you are: UNSUPPORTED.
- **Key line:** "For self-directed flashcard use and academic performance, certainty is very low." [E039]
- **Sources:** E039, E035

## Self-control is a battery, and using it up makes the next hard thing harder

- **Grade:** WEAK
- **What the research shows:** A preregistered 36-lab test found no significant effect, d = 0.06, CI -0.02 to 0.14, N = 2461, with the data 4.4 times more likely under the null. An unplanned full-sample analysis gave d = 0.08 (E020). A commentary argues an earlier 23-lab null used a weak task, but that re-analysis was post hoc (E021). All brief lab tasks, mostly students.
- **Where it overreaches:** After a draining social moment your willpower is spent: UNSUPPORTED, no social test. Ego depletion is proven false: UNSUPPORTED, since a small effect is not ruled out (E020).
- **Key line:** "The general conclusion is that the depletion effect is likely small (including zero)" [E020]
- **Sources:** E020, E021

## Feeling mentally tired makes your next effortful task worse

- **Grade:** MIXED
- **What the research shows:** The small depletion effect was larger in people who reported more fatigue, but that was exploratory (E020). A similar effort-linked pattern was post hoc and may be circular, since it splits people by their own ratings (E021). A review says felt fatigue and measured performance often do not line up (E040). All lab, none social.
- **Where it overreaches:** You have a social battery that measurably runs out: UNSUPPORTED. No source tests it.
- **Key line:** "confidence in them should be low until they are replicated" [E020]
- **Sources:** E020, E021, E040

## Short breaks restore your focus during long mental work

- **Grade:** MIXED
- **What the research shows:** One narrative review reports lab studies where rest helped and a field study where any break helped, but gives no pooled effect, no study count, and calls the methods underdeveloped (E040). Mostly lab tasks with students.
- **Where it overreaches:** The ideal break is 3 to 10 minutes: WEAK, a rule of thumb and not a trial result. Nature walks restore attention best: WEAK. Scrolling your phone counts as rest: UNSUPPORTED, since the review gives no data (E040).
- **Key line:** "there is a chaotic plentitude of theoretical proclamations available but hardly any testable model" [E040]
- **Sources:** E040

## Pure rest beats switching to a different task

- **Grade:** WEAK
- **What the research shows:** One lab study reported by E040 found performance best after rest, worst with no break, and in between for a change of task. No effect size is given in E040, and a field study it also reports found break type mattered less than taking a break at all.
- **Where it overreaches:** Switching tasks gives you no recovery: UNSUPPORTED. The reported study places it between rest and no break.
- **Key line:** "performance was best for rest, worst for the continuous condition" [E040] (reported from another study)
- **Sources:** E040

## Switching back and forth costs you, and knowing that will make you stop

- **Grade:** MIXED
- **What the research shows:** A narrative paper reports that dual-task and switching lab work generally finds costs on one or both tasks, depending on timing, difficulty, and similarity (E041). No pooled estimate and no new data. Everyday workplace switching is not measured here.
- **Where it overreaches:** Teaching people the cost makes them multitask less: UNSUPPORTED. E041 reports education has been unsuccessful so far.
- **Key line:** "however, these interventions have been unsuccessful to date, possibly because effectively altering beliefs requires long-term management" [E041]
- **Sources:** E041

## Heavy media multitasking damages your attention

- **Grade:** WEAK
- **What the research shows:** E041 reports inconsistent studies with small effects and high heterogeneity. A cross-sectional study of 214 young Italian adults found no significant link to attention control, working memory, or fluid reasoning, and one borderline link to lower associative thinking, p = 0.049 (E042). No causal direction.
- **Where it overreaches:** Media multitasking is proven harmless: UNSUPPORTED. The E042 authors say their nulls do not show that.
- **Key line:** "Overall, the correlations between the MMI and cognitive measures were weak and largely non-significant." [E042]
- **Sources:** E041, E042

## People like you more than you think after a conversation

- **Grade:** MIXED
- **What the research shows:** The gap appeared in all five studies of one paper. Lab: b = -0.65, -0.57, -0.38 on a 7-point scale. Everyday: workshop strangers, and first-year dorm mates over months (b = -0.36), with no gap at the last time point (E022). E043 reports the same underestimate in passing. This is the strongest finding here, but it is one team, not preregistered, with no bias correction, and low-shy people showed no significant gap.
- **Where it overreaches:** Everyone always likes you more than you think, including close friends and high-stakes talks: UNSUPPORTED. Knowing about the gap removes the fear: UNSUPPORTED, not tested.
- **Key line:** "people systematically underestimated how much their conversation partners liked them and enjoyed their company" [E022]
- **Sources:** E022, E043

## The liking gap is biggest for shy people

- **Grade:** WEAK
- **What the research shows:** In one lab study of 36 people, split into groups of about 10, the gap was 1.14 for the shyest, 0.55 for average, and 0.25 (not significant) for the least shy, interaction p = 0.003 (E022). It was not tested in the later studies. Introversion was not measured, and shyness is not the same thing.
- **Where it overreaches:** Introverts have the biggest liking gap: UNSUPPORTED. No source tests it.
- **Key line:** "In sum, shyness moderated the liking gap" [E022]
- **Sources:** E022

## Other people see you more kindly than you see yourself

- **Grade:** WEAK
- **What the research shows:** In a lab sample of 15 videos, trained coders' ratings predicted how much partners actually liked someone, and did not significantly predict how liked the person felt (E022). The claim about people with social anxiety disorder comes from earlier work reported by E023 and was not tested there. Harsh self-focused thoughts carried part of the gap, a correlational result in one study of 84 students (E022).
- **Where it overreaches:** You always come across fine: UNSUPPORTED. Assigned avoidance lowered partner liking (E023).
- **Key line:** "In general, people with SAD come across more positively than they think" [E023]
- **Sources:** E022, E023

## Nobody noticed that as much as you think (the spotlight effect)

- **Grade:** MIXED
- **What the research shows:** Not settled by these sources. The corpus has no direct test of the spotlight effect, meaning how much others notice a person's slip or appearance. E022 mentions it only through other work. The nearest evidence, the liking gap, measures how much people are liked, not how much they are noticed (E022).
- **Where it overreaches:** Research proves no one noticed your mistake: UNSUPPORTED. Do not stretch the liking gap to cover it, and do not call the effect false.
- **Key line:** "Our studies suggest that after people have conversations, they are liked more than they know." [E022] (about liking, not noticing)
- **Sources:** E022

## Talking to a stranger gives you more than you expect

- **Grade:** MIXED
- **What the research shows:** Across seven experiments in parks, labs, and online, people learned more from 10-minute talks with strangers than they predicted (for example b = 1.14, p < 0.001), and enjoyment and liking were also underestimated (E043). Partners had agreed to talk, and a train study of very short chats, reported by E043, did not show it. No trait analysis.
- **Where it overreaches:** Introverts especially underestimate how good contact will feel: WEAK. That rests on an unnumbered lab page (E013) and on other work reported by E012.
- **Key line:** "people systematically undervalue what they might learn in conversation, anticipating that they will learn less than they actually do." [E043]
- **Sources:** E043, E013, E012

## Claims this corpus cannot grade

No source here speaks to panoramic vision, morning light, caffeine timing, non-sleep deep rest, the arrow model of focus, challenge networks, task conflict, or the four mental modes. If a seat leans on one, say so. Do not grade it or call it false.
