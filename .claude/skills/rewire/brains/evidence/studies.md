# Evidence studies

One entry per source. Effect sizes, samples, and limits come from the extraction notes. A study mentioned only inside another source is marked "reported by E0xx" and has not been read directly.

## E003: Safety behaviors and social anxiety (Kim, 2022)

- **Type:** Observational (cross-sectional online self-report survey, not an experiment).
- **Sample:** 487 adults in South Korea, ages 19 to 59 (M = 39.58), 239 women. 260 scored high and 227 low on social anxiety, with the middle of the range left out. Community sample, not confirmed clinical. Setting: online survey about everyday behavior, no lab task.
- **Finding:** In the high group, avoidance (beta = 0.36, p < 0.001) and anxiety-symptom control (beta = 0.21, p < 0.001) predicted social anxiety. Impression management did not (beta = 0.05, p = 0.448). The serial path through state and then trait post-event processing (mentally replaying the event) was significant for impression management (beta = 0.13, p = 0.041) and avoidance (beta = 0.11, p = 0.005), not anxiety-symptom control (beta = 0.03, p = 0.246). Single mediators alone were not significant. In the low group only avoidance predicted social anxiety (beta = 0.22, p = 0.005), and that effect was not significant once post-event processing was included.
- **Limits:** One time point, so no causal direction for safety behaviors or replay. The extreme-group design inflates group differences. Depression and other anxiety disorders were not controlled. No introvert analysis, and replication is needed.

## E004: Mental contrasting with if-then plans (Wang, 2021)

- **Type:** Meta-analysis of field intervention studies (labelled a systematic review article by the journal).
- **Sample:** 21 articles, 24 effect sizes, 15,907 participants, of whom two large online studies supply 13,909 (87.44%). General adults, college students, adult patients, and children, mostly Western. Includes 5 unpublished theses. Setting: field studies only, lab-only experiments excluded.
- **Finding:** Small to medium effect on goal attainment, g = 0.336, 95% CI 0.229 to 0.443, I2 = 59.321%. Trim-and-fill lowered it to g = 0.242, 95% CI 0.143 to 0.342. Egger's test pointed to possible publication bias (t = 5.46, p < 0.01). Experimenter delivery (g = 0.465) beat document delivery (g = 0.277), Q(1) = 4.797, p < 0.05. Publication status, sample age, goal domain, measure type, and expectation of success were not significant moderators. Individual studies ranged from g = -0.11 to 1.14.
- **Limits:** No social fear or conversation outcomes and no personality analysis. Few studies for moderator tests. The authors cannot say whether the combination beats either part alone. Follow-up length was not tested.

## E009: Breathwork programs for stress (Fincham, 2023)

- **Type:** Meta-analysis of RCTs (preregistered).
- **Sample:** Stress outcome: 12 RCTs, 785 adults (417 breathwork, 368 control), mean age 41.7, 75% female. 6 physical health samples, 5 nonclinical, 1 mental health. Secondary outcomes: anxiety k = 20, depression k = 18. Setting: multi-session programs in everyday life or clinics, not lab provocation and not acute use.
- **Finding:** Less self-reported stress than controls, g = -0.35, 95% CI -0.55 to -0.14, p = 0.0009, I2 = 42%. Anxiety g = -0.32, CI -0.48 to -0.16, I2 = 51%. Depression g = -0.40, CI -0.58 to -0.22, I2 = 58%. Null results: no significant dose-response for practice time (p = 0.418), sessions (p = 0.107), or frequency (p = 0.224). Stress effects were not significant in physical health samples, the one mental health study, or fast-paced breathing (2 studies), though differences between subgroups were not significant either.
- **Limits:** All outcomes self-report and no physiology. Risk of bias: 3 high, 9 some concerns, none low. No follow-up analysis was possible and specific techniques were not analysed. Egger's test was non-significant (p = 0.947), though the funnel plot looked skewed by small studies. The first author is a trained Breath Teacher (declared).

## E012: Acting extraverted and introverted for a week (Margolis, 2020)

- **Type:** RCT (within-person crossover with order randomized, no neutral control group).
- **Sample:** 131 undergraduates at one public university, 69% female, mean age 19.2, 46% Asian, 34% Latino(a). Not selected for introversion. Non-clinical. Setting: everyday life, online, self-report outcomes only.
- **Finding:** Positive affect grew in the extraversion week (Days 8-15 0.67, CI 0.37 to 0.97; Days 1-8 0.30, CI 0.00 to 0.60) and fell in the introversion week (Days 1-8 -0.56, CI -0.81 to -0.31; Days 8-15 -0.54, CI -0.85 to -0.23), in Week 1 SD units. Connectedness, competence, autonomy, and flow also moved. Effects on negative affect, happiness, and life satisfaction were weaker. Happiness was not significant (F = 1.37, p = .26) and the negative affect effect of acting introverted differed by order. Baseline trait extraversion did not moderate the effects. Trait scores shifted too, but the authors caution against reading that as personality change.
- **Limits:** Both prompts said the behavior was beneficial, so growth may be biased upwards. One week per condition. Authenticity, fatigue, and cognitive cost were not measured, and no separate cost for introverts was tested, so this study was not designed to detect such costs. Longer-term costs are left open by the authors. The claim that introverts paid no cost for acting extraverted comes from Zelenski, Santoro and Whelan 2012, reported by E012, and is not a finding of this study. One other trial, reported by E012, found extraverts benefited more.

## E013: Should introverts act more extraverted? (no author listed, CU Happiness Laboratory, date not given)

- **Type:** Lab web page. A secondary summary of the lab's own studies, not a paper. No methods or statistics.
- **Sample:** Not reported. A lab group-task study is described only as people invited to the lab. Setting: short lab session, non-clinical, plus unspecified momentary behavior work.
- **Finding:** No effect sizes given. The page says acting extraverted goes with higher reported happiness for both trait introverts and extraverts, and that there was no Stroop cost for introverts acting extraverted, while trait extraverts performed worse after acting introverted. Unpleasant emotions showed no personality differences. Trait introverts reported feeling more authentic acting extraverted than acting introverted, in the moment.
- **Limits:** Nothing is checkable: no sample, no numbers, and the underlying papers are not in this source set. Fatigue was only tapped through a Stroop task after a short task. Nothing about weeks of sustained acting. The page itself says strongly identified introverts may differ and that more research is needed.

## E014: Defending a reconsolidation finding (Walker, 2016)

- **Type:** Commentary (a letter in PNAS by Walker and Stickgold replying to E015). Not a study. No new data.
- **Sample:** Not reported. No participants of its own. It discusses E015 and the authors' own 2003 finger-sequence study. Setting: lab motor task, no everyday-life relevance.
- **Finding:** The authors argue the failed replication in E015 may reflect differences in time of day, participant age (18-52 y vs 18-27 y), and task instructions. They say their original result had a moderate effect (d = 0.59) and power of 69%, so a similar-sized replication would miss it about one time in three. They also state that almost two dozen human studies and over 900 animal studies report reconsolidation, a count they assert and do not test.
- **Limits:** An argument, not a test. The proposed boundary conditions are offered as possible explanations, and the authors are defending their own work. Cannot be graded as evidence on its own.

## E015: Failed replications of memory updating (Hardwicke, 2016)

- **Type:** Replication (seven experiments in one paper: four direct, three conceptual).
- **Sample:** Direct: 64 people (16 per experiment, 49 females, median age 22). Conceptual: 48 people (16 per experiment, 38 females, median age 22). University College London subject pool, right-handed, no reported neurological, psychiatric, or sleep disorder. Setting: lab, three-day protocol, finger-tap and number or letter sequences.
- **Finding:** Null. The key drop in old-sequence accuracy did not appear: accuracy declined by less than 1% on average, compared with about 57% in the original study. Pooled across experiments 1-4, BF01 = 5.743 for accuracy and 36.027 for speed, favoring the null. In the conceptual replications the no-reminder group recalled less, not more (1.0 to 0.488, versus 1.0 to 0.638 with a reminder), the opposite of what the theory predicts. Age 18-27 re-analysis (n = 48) also showed no impairment (P = 0.092).
- **Limits:** Sequence tasks with 16 people per experiment and university volunteers. No emotional, fearful, or social memories, drugs, or therapy. The original's statistics were estimated from plots. A null cannot by itself prove absence, though the authors estimate combined power at 0.99.

## E020: Multi-site test of ego depletion (Vohs, 2021)

- **Type:** Replication (preregistered multi-lab study with frequentist and Bayesian meta-analysis of lab results).
- **Sample:** 36 labs, N = 3531, mean age 20.92, mostly students, mostly U.S. Preregistered exclusions removed 1068 people (30.25%), leaving about 2461 for confirmatory tests. Non-clinical. Setting: lab, brief tasks.
- **Finding:** Null on the main test: d = 0.06, 95% CI -0.02 to 0.14. Bayesian analysis: data 4.4 times more likely under the null than under depletion (posterior median 0.08, CI 0.01 to 0.16). Exploratory full sample: small significant d = 0.08, with Bayesian tests inconclusive. Figure tracing duration was the one significant outcome (d = 0.15, CI 0.02 to 0.29). Cognitive Estimation Test d = 0.01, CI -0.10 to 0.12. Exploratory: larger in people reporting more fatigue. Trait self-control and willpower beliefs did not moderate it.
- **Limits:** Only four tasks. Exclusions removed nearly a third of the sample. Small-effect and fatigue results are exploratory. It does not show that fatigue never hurts performance or that willpower is unlimited. Nothing about real-life social situations or introverts.

## E021: Critique of a failed ego depletion replication (Dang, 2016)

- **Type:** Commentary with a non-preregistered, exploratory re-analysis of another project's data. Not a new study.
- **Sample:** Hagger and Chatzisarantis 2016, reported by E021: 23 labs, N = 2141. The re-analysis pooled N = 2058. Population not stated. Setting: lab, non-clinical.
- **Finding:** The author argues the E-crossing task may not have been depleting: conditions did not differ on fatigue (t = 1.63, p = 0.104, d = 0.07). In the depletion condition, people reporting more effort did worse later (condition by effort interaction: reaction time beta = 0.10, p = 0.001; variability beta = 0.08, p = 0.004). Interactions with the other three check items were not significant.
- **Limits:** Post-hoc re-analysis by a critic of a null result. Splitting people by how effortful they say a task felt can create an effect from self-report alone. It cites an unpublished meta-analysis that cannot be checked. It does not show depletion is real.

## E022: The liking gap in conversations (Boothby, 2018)

- **Type:** Observational and experimental lab and field studies (five studies in one paper, not a meta-analysis, several not preregistered).
- **Sample:** Study 1a: 36 community members. Study 1b: 15 videos from 1a. Study 2: 84 Yale students and recent graduates. Study 3: 102 from a Harvard subject pool. Study 4: 100 analyzed of 118 at UK talk-to-strangers workshops. Study 5: 102 first-year Yale students in dorm suites. Setting: lab (1a, 1b, 2, 3), workshop (4), real dorm relationships over an academic year (5). Non-clinical.
- **Finding:** People rated liking their partner more than they thought the partner liked them, in every study. Study 1a b = -0.65, CI -0.87 to -0.42. Study 2 b = -0.57, CI -0.71 to -0.44. Study 3 b = -0.38, CI -0.59 to -0.18. Study 5 b = -0.36, CI -0.47 to -0.24, with no significant gap at the final time point (p = .87). Longer conversations did not shrink it. Negative self-thoughts mediated part of it (b = -0.14, CI -0.22 to -0.07). Shyness moderated it in Study 1a only (b = 0.03, p = .003): high shyness 1.14, low shyness 0.25 with CI -0.15 to 0.65, not significant.
- **Limits:** Averages only, with no significant gap in the low-shy group. Mostly students and mild settings. Mediation is correlational. The shyness result rests on small tertiles in one study. Introversion was not measured, and shyness is not introversion. Whether telling people about the gap helps was not tested.

## E023: Avoidance and impression management as safety behaviors (Gray, 2019)

- **Type:** RCT (Study 2, lab experiment) plus observational (Study 1, clinic questionnaire and factor analysis).
- **Sample:** Study 1: 106 people with social anxiety disorder (SAD), 28 with PTSD, 59 non-patient controls, UK specialist clinics. Study 2: 96 non-clinical undergraduates (mean age 20.80, 58 female). Setting: Study 1 clinic questionnaires, Study 2 lab, 5-minute conversations between strangers.
- **Finding:** Study 2: both behaviors raised the performer's own anxiety (b = 9.08, p = .003). Avoidance also led partners to rate the performer as more anxious (b = 18.75, p < .001) and like them less (b = -11.79, p < .001), and both people enjoyed the talk less (b = -19.25, p < .001). Impression management had no significant effect on partner ratings of anxiety, partner liking, enjoyment, or desire to repeat. Self-focus showed no significant effects. Study 1: safety behaviors were highest in SAD (M = 1.43), then PTSD (1.03), then controls (0.77), p < .001.
- **Limits:** One short lab conversation in students, instructed rather than habitual use, with no active control. The experimental group had higher baseline anxiety despite randomization. Performers complied 64.12% of the time. Nothing about lasting anxiety or introverts.

## E024: VR exposure therapy for social anxiety disorder (Morina, 2021)

- **Type:** Meta-analysis (a short re-analysis and critique of an earlier meta-analysis, Horigome et al. 2020, reported by E024; not a full standalone systematic review).
- **Sample:** 12 studies (six RCTs, six uncontrolled). Patients with social anxiety disorder, including some with public speaking anxiety. 228 across 12 arms pre to post, 222 across 5 arms versus waitlist, 272 across 7 arms versus in vivo exposure. Setting: clinical or treatment-seeking samples in controlled therapy.
- **Finding:** Versus waitlist: g = 0.88, 95% CI 0.43 to 1.34, I2 = 61.34%, prediction interval 0.03 to 1.79. Versus in vivo exposure, no significant difference: g = 0.07, CI -0.32 to 0.17, p = 0.562 (sign as printed), and at longest follow-up g = -0.24, CI -0.83 to 0.35, p = 0.426. Uncontrolled pre to post g = 1.20, CI 0.91 to 1.50, which the authors say does not account for time.
- **Limits:** Few RCTs and moderate to high heterogeneity. No significant difference is not proof of equivalence. Patients in therapy, so this does not cover non-clinical introverts or self-run practice. The authors found the earlier review overstated the evidence.

## E025: Stand-alone VR exposure (Rejbrand, 2023)

- **Type:** Meta-analysis with systematic review (registered, RCTs only).
- **Sample:** 6 studies evaluated, 5 pooled, 28 to 97 people each, median ages 23 to 42, majority female, Western countries. People with social phobia, SAD, or high questionnaire scores, recruited by ads, flyers, and referrals. Authors describe them as mainly non-clinical people with symptoms. Setting: controlled trials of VR or AR, not real-life outcomes.
- **Finding:** Five-study pooling: SMD -0.82, 95% CI -1.52 to -0.13, GRADE moderate. Null on the three-study single-scale analysis: mean difference -6.88 points, CI -17.31 to 3.55, not significant, heterogeneity high, GRADE low. One 4 to 6 year follow-up found no significant difference from exposure group therapy, with no no-treatment comparison.
- **Limits:** Few small trials. High risk of attrition and reporting bias in most. No publication bias assessment. No pooled real-life behavior outcome. Applicability to non-clinical people is uncertain.

## E026: Social anxiety and fear conditioning (Wake, 2024)

- **Type:** Meta-analysis with systematic review of lab conditioning experiments. Not a treatment trial. Not registered.
- **Sample:** 21 articles, 23 experiments, mostly adults, mostly German and US. Acquisition: 14 experiments, 454 healthy controls and 399 socially anxious people. Extinction: 7 experiments, 231 healthy controls and 193 people with SAD. Setting: lab Pavlovian conditioning.
- **Finding:** Null. Threat learning: d = -0.009, 95% CI -0.212 to 0.195, p = .934. Extinction (learning safety): d = 0.047, CI -0.131 to 0.224, p = .607. Heterogeneity was significant in both. Clinical versus trait samples did not differ. Publication bias evidence was limited (p = .696).
- **Limits:** Tests no treatment, so it says nothing direct about whether exposure works. Lab social stimuli may not create real evaluation fear. Many experiments could not be pooled. The authors' suggestion that negative beliefs matter more is interpretation, not a tested result. Nothing about introverts.

## E027: Interventions for shy and socially anxious children (Cordier, 2021)

- **Type:** Meta-analysis with systematic review.
- **Sample:** 25 studies, 1,895 children aged 6 to 12, 20 pooled, 18 with a control group. 13 studies described typically developing children, others had diagnoses including social phobia. Nine countries. Setting: clinic, school, and other settings, mostly outside the classroom.
- **Finding:** Versus comparison groups: g = 0.82, 95% CI 0.5 to 1.14, I2 = 85.1%. Of 18 studies, 6 large, 1 moderate, 7 small, 4 negligible. Pre to post g = 1.21, CI 0.87 to 1.54, I2 = 86.2%, with no control group. Null subgroups: individual sessions only (g = 0.32, p = 0.068), child and parent together (g = 1.01, CI -0.28 to 2.3), and parent-, self-, and teacher-rated outcomes. Clinician-rated g = 0.95 was significant. No sign of publication bias.
- **Limits:** Children only, so no conclusions for adults. No evidence of long-term benefit on later social anxiety disorder. Very high heterogeneity, mixed diagnoses, and no active ingredient identified. Says nothing about introversion or temperament.

## E028: If-then plans and drinking (Cooke, 2023)

- **Type:** Meta-analysis (preregistered systematic review).
- **Sample:** 16 studies. 27 comparisons for weekly drinking, 20 for heavy episodic drinking. Arms of 18 to 93, mean ages 16.62 to 39.54, 19 university and 8 community samples, mostly England. Total N not reported. Non-clinical. Setting: everyday drinking, self-reported, 1 to 24 weeks.
- **Finding:** Weekly drinking: small effect, d+ = -0.14, 95% CI -0.24 to -0.03, I2 = 45.09. Heavy episodic drinking: null, d+ = -0.01, CI -0.10 to 0.08. Community samples d+ = -0.38, CI -0.58 to -0.18. University samples d+ = -0.04, CI -0.13 to 0.05, not significant. Online delivery d+ = -0.04, CI -0.14 to 0.06, not significant. Standard if-then format d+ = -0.07, CI -0.16 to 0.02, not significant. Effects shrank with longer follow-up. No publication bias detected.
- **Limits:** All studies at high risk of bias. Self-report only. Sample type and delivery mode are confounded. Mostly female and English samples. Drinking only, so this does not size if-then plans in other domains.

## E029: If-then plans and physical activity (da Silva, 2018)

- **Type:** Systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs (preregistered).
- **Sample:** 13 RCTs, 11 pooled (12 effect sizes), 30 to 709 people each, ages 18 to 76. Total N not reported. 77% specific health populations, 3 studies healthy adults. Setting: everyday physical activity, all self-reported, 2 to 32 weeks.
- **Finding:** Null overall: 0.15, 95% CI -0.01 to 0.31, I2 = 54%. Plans reinforced during follow-up, a post hoc subgroup: 0.25, CI 0.05 to 0.45. The non-reinforced subgroup was not reported as significant.
- **Limits:** Self-report, with 54% non-validated questionnaires. Publication bias not tested. The reinforcement split was not a planned main test. Mostly clinical or at-risk adults. No social outcomes and no personality analysis.

## E030: If-then plans in children (Breitwieser, 2026)

- **Type:** Meta-analysis (registered report, systematic review).
- **Sample:** 42 studies, 52 effect sizes, N = 12,957 children, mean age 10.67, range 4.5 to 12.99. 12 effect sizes clinical (11 ADHD), 40 non-clinical. 31 effect sizes from Germany. Setting: mostly lab tasks (32 effect sizes), 20 field.
- **Finding:** g = 0.31, 95% CI 0.21 to 0.41, I2 = 65.2%. Egger's test significant (p = .003). Corrected estimates depend on method: PET-PEESE 0.15, selection model 0.38. Excluding 7 outliers gave g = 0.18. Younger children gained more (b = -0.05 per year, p = .022). Clinical versus non-clinical was not significant in the main model (p = .075). Number of plans, experimenter help, and time to measurement were not significant.
- **Limits:** Children only, no direct transfer to adults. Risk of bias for assignment effects: 2 low, 27 some concerns, 23 high. Moderator tests were underpowered. Most outcomes measured within 24 hours. Only one study used an internal cue such as a feeling, and few tested interpersonal goals. Several studies found no effect in typical children.

## E031: Stress reappraisal and task performance (Bosshard, 2024)

- **Type:** Meta-analysis of RCTs (preregistered).
- **Sample:** 35 studies, 44 effect sizes, N = 5638, 65.1% female, weighted mean age 22.6, mostly students. No clinical samples. 9 effect sizes from dissertations and theses. Setting: short-term lab and classroom tasks.
- **Finding:** Small effect on performance: d = 0.23, 95% CI 0.14 to 0.33, I2 = 52.6%, prediction interval -0.18 to 0.65. Publication bias signs: trim and fill cut it to d = 0.14, CI 0.04 to 0.24, Egger's p = 0.042, and the p-curve was inconclusive. Reappraisal alone d = 0.22, CI 0.11 to 0.32. Stress-is-enhancing mindset alone was not significant, d = 0.18, CI -0.15 to 0.51. Public tasks d = 0.34 versus written d = 0.20 was only a trend (p = 0.064). Mixed interventions d = 0.45 (k = 5), which the authors say may be confounded.
- **Limits:** The outcome is performance, not felt anxiety. No trait or social anxiety tests. Few long-term data. An earlier meta-analysis, reported by E031, found a small effect on subjective stress and no significant effect on physiology. Public tasks include golf putting and darts as well as speech tasks.

## E032: Reappraisal before breaking bad news (Bosshard, 2025)

- **Type:** RCT (preregistered, 2 x 2 design).
- **Sample:** 229 third-year medical students from four Swiss universities, 69% female, mean age 22.42. Cardiovascular data complete for 189. Healthy volunteers. Setting: lab, simulated consultation with a trained actor.
- **Finding:** Primary cardiovascular index improved with reappraisal (M = 0.46 vs -0.44, beta = 0.90, p < 0.001). Null on the self-report primary: resources-demands evaluation not significantly changed (beta = 0.37, p = 0.087). The cardiovascular effect held for reappraisal alone versus no intervention (1.31, p < 0.001) but not when added to a worked example (0.47, p = 0.070). A worked example improved self-rated resources (beta = 0.62, p = 0.005) but not the cardiovascular index.
- **Limits:** One population and a simulated task. Performance is not reported in this paper. Self-rated state stayed in threat across all periods. Data lost for 40 of 229. No trait analysis. A challenge pattern does not equal a better experience or outcome.

## E033: Five-minute daily breathing versus mindfulness (Balban, 2023)

- **Type:** RCT (remote, exploratory, registered retrospectively, not preregistered).
- **Sample:** 108 enrolled (24 mindfulness, 30 cyclic sighing, 21 box breathing, 33 cyclic hyperventilation), adults mostly from a Stanford undergraduate psychology class. Moderate to severe psychiatric or medical conditions excluded. Setting: everyday life, remote, 5 minutes a day for 28 days.
- **Finding:** Null against mindfulness on state anxiety and negative affect: all groups dropped (state anxiety mindfulness -3.95, breathwork -3.03). Breathwork raised positive affect more than mindfulness (+1.91 vs +1.22), and only cyclic sighing was significantly higher than mindfulness. Respiratory rate fell more with breathwork (p < 0.05). No significant changes in heart rate variability, resting heart rate, sleep, or trait anxiety.
- **Limits:** Exploratory study with small groups and no passive control. Ratings were taken around calm daily practice, not a stressful event. No follow-up. Mostly university students. Andrew Huberman, a corresponding author, recorded the instruction videos. The senior author led the study and became a WHOOP advisor after data collection (declared). The trial did not use the term physiological sigh.

## E034: Breathing practices framework (Bentley, 2023)

- **Type:** Systematic review, no pooled meta-analysis, no registered protocol. Vote count of effective versus ineffective interventions, no effect sizes.
- **Sample:** 58 clinical studies (41 RCTs, 17 non-randomized), 72 interventions, 5407 participants, mean age 35.9, 43.92% female. Youth, healthy adults, high-anxiety adults, acute and chronic clinical, and simulated-stress settings. Setting: 29 of 58 in hospital or clinic, 15 at universities.
- **Finding:** 54 of 72 interventions (75%) significantly reduced stress or anxiety, 18 did not. Simulated-stress settings, the closest to performance anxiety, were least successful: 5 of 11 (45%). Sessions under 5 minutes: six of nine ineffective. Single-session: 10 of 23 effective. Human-guided first-session training (p < 0.001) and multiple sessions (p < 0.001) were associated with effectiveness. Population (p = 0.14), study design (p = 0.77), group versus individual practice (p = 0.79), and at-home practice (p = 0.24) were not.
- **Limits:** Size of benefit unknown. Study quality described by the authors as mediocre-at-best: 7 RCTs and 0 non-RCTs rated good. Associations, not causes. Publication bias not assessed. No comparison of single techniques and no introversion analysis.

## E035: Volitional personality change (Haehner, 2024)

- **Type:** Systematic review with a small random-effects meta-analysis inside it (preregistered search).
- **Sample:** 30 longitudinal studies, N = 7719, partly overlapping samples, 52% US, 62% college-student samples, mean age 25.57, 67.69% female. The intervention meta-analysis used 7 studies, 26 effect sizes. Non-clinical. Setting: lab and everyday-life designs, including online programs, coaching, and an app.
- **Finding:** Pre-post change in the desired direction: d = 0.22, 95% CI 0.005 to 0.433, p = 0.046. Extraversion d = 0.38, CI 0.078 to 0.680 (3 effect sizes). Conscientiousness d = 0.31, CI 0.049 to 0.571. Intervals include zero for neuroticism (d = 0.33, CI -0.171 to 0.830), agreeableness (d = 0.15, CI -0.229 to 0.521), and openness (d = 0.21, CI -0.469 to 0.879). Exploratory follow-up d = 0.37, CI 0.140 to 0.591. The mere goal to change was only weakly related to actual change, and some interventions did not work or backfired, including one reported control group that changed more than the intervention group.
- **Limits:** Pre-post estimates, because few studies reported control-group effects. No risk of bias assessment. No moderator analysis. Mostly self-report, with demand effects possible. No introvert split. It does not show that introverts can or should become extraverts.

## E036: Extraverted behavior and immune gene expression (Martinez, 2026)

- **Type:** RCT.
- **Sample:** 119 people from a university campus community (87% undergraduate), mean age 21.51, 78% female, 46% Hispanic/Latinx, 36% Asian. 59 extraversion, 60 control. Self-selected, not screened for introversion. Non-clinical. Setting: everyday life, 6-week protocol, dried blood spots.
- **Finding:** Null on the pre-registered primary test: t(227) = 0.38, p = 0.705. Secondary pathway analysis: inflammatory NF-kB down (0.67-fold, p = 0.028), antiviral IRF up (1.23-fold, p = 0.016), CREB down (0.86-fold, p = 0.005). Loneliness fell more in the extraversion group (2.38 to 2.06, control 2.52 to 2.52). Null at 1-month follow-up (composite p = 0.157), with loneliness back near baseline (2.33).
- **Limits:** Positive results are from a secondary analysis the study was not powered for. Authors say to treat them with caution until replicated. No immune function or disease measure. Trait introversion was never examined. The publication year is taken from article metadata.

## E037: Failed replication of memory modification (van Schie, 2017)

- **Type:** Replication (single study, partial failure to replicate).
- **Sample:** 96 students (48 male, 48 female, mean age 21.2), randomized to four groups. Mental disorder, drug abuse, and medication excluded. Setting: lab, recognition of emotional and neutral pictures over 9 days.
- **Finding:** The critical comparison, reactivation plus new learning against new learning alone, was weak: BF10 = 1.99 overall, 1.17 negative, 2.66 neutral, called anecdotal by the authors. Mean accuracy: reactivation plus new learning 63.96%, new learning alone 65.97%, reactivation alone 73.19%, neither 76.39%. The original, Wichert et al. 2013a, is reported by E037.
- **Limits:** Simple lab-made memories, unlike clinical or social ones. The authors cannot say whether this is a false negative or the original a false positive. Interference alone can explain the result without reconsolidation. It does not show reconsolidation is false.

## E038: Publication bias in rodent reconsolidation work (Schroyens, 2021)

- **Type:** Animal review. A preregistered systematic review of rodent studies with meta-analytic tests for publication bias. No human data.
- **Sample:** Rats and mice. 95 published drug-vehicle comparisons (N = 1896 animals), four drugs, plus 12 unpublished comparisons from seven researchers in five groups. Setting: animal lab.
- **Finding:** About 80% of published comparisons were significant versus 20% of unpublished ones (p < 0.001). Funnel plot asymmetric (Egger's t(60) = 5.04, p < 0.001). Heterogeneity I2 = 75.26%. In the authors' own exact replications, the midazolam effect appeared in only two of seven runs, those where the original experimenter worked in the original lab.
- **Limits:** Says nothing about people or therapy. Tests only drug-induced amnesia for contextual fear. No corrected effect size. Unpublished data were limited and the comparison exploratory. The authors do not claim the phenomenon is absent.

## E039: Spaced repetition and exams in medical students (Luu, 2026)

- **Type:** Systematic review (narrative synthesis, no meta-analysis, not registered).
- **Sample:** 21 studies of undergraduate medical students, 29 to 684 learners each, mostly preclinical. 6 controlled (3 randomized), 15 observational. Setting: real course and national exams, not a lab.
- **Finding:** Effect direction positive in 10 studies, mixed in 5, neutral in 5 (the paper's counts total 20 of 21). No negative effects reported. One quasi-experimental study had g = 1.53 and 0.90 against two controls. Durrani d = 0.8, CI not reported. Observational studies of self-chosen flashcard use were mixed, with several null results. 12 of 21 studies gave no effect size or interval.
- **Limits:** No study at low risk of bias. Certainty low for structured retrieval, very low for self-directed flashcards. Single reviewer. Few studies separate spacing from retrieval. Usage was learner-chosen where more use went with better scores. Factual exam material only, so no transfer to social skills or fear.

## E040: Rest breaks and attention (Schumann, 2022)

- **Type:** Narrative review of theory and methods, labelled a systematic review by the journal. No pooled effect sizes and no stated search method.
- **Sample:** Not reported. No study count or participants. Studies discussed are mostly lab with students, plus some field studies with employees using self-report. Non-clinical.
- **Finding:** The authors say a review by effect size or moderator was impossible because the field is too heterogeneous and methods are weak. They offer a rule of thumb that 3 to 10 minute rests suit 30 to 120 minutes of mental work, drawn from the literature and not a trial result. Reported by E040, with no effect sizes: Helton and Russell 2015 found performance best after rest and worst with no break, and Scholz et al. 2017 found break type mattered less than taking a break. Waking rest and consolidation findings are described as mixed.
- **Limits:** A secondary summary with no quality ratings and no publication bias discussion. Felt fatigue and measured performance do not always line up. Nothing on social anxiety, exposure, or introverts.

## E041: A model of media multitasking (Drody, 2025)

- **Type:** Commentary (a narrative theoretical review proposing a model, not a systematic review, not preregistered, no new data).
- **Sample:** Not reported. No participants. A count of 72 articles from a PubMed search is coded by model component. Setting: draws on lab and everyday-life studies by others.
- **Finding:** All reported by E041 from other work. Habitual media multitasking questionnaires give inconsistent links to cognitive control, and recent meta-analyses show small effects with high heterogeneity. Dual-task costs depend on timing, difficulty, and task similarity. Educating people about multitasking costs has not worked to date.
- **Limits:** The five-component model is an untested proposal. No effect sizes of its own and no intervention tested. It does not show that multitasking causes attention problems or fatigue. No introvert versus extravert comparison.

## E042: Media multitasking and cognition (Caprì, 2026)

- **Type:** Observational (cross-sectional, correlational).
- **Sample:** 214 healthy Italian adults aged 18 to 30 (M = 22.77), 108 women, 106 men, one university, mostly students. Psychiatric, neurological, sleep, and attention disorders excluded. Setting: self-reported everyday habit plus lab cognitive tests, one time point.
- **Finding:** Mostly null. No significant link to the combined cognitive outcomes (p = 0.089), working memory (beta = -0.118, p = 0.097), fluid reasoning (beta = -0.032, p = 0.658), or attention control. No significant difference between heavy and light multitaskers. One borderline result: lower Remote Associates scores, beta = -0.141, 95% CI -0.141 to -0.000, p = 0.049.
- **Limits:** No causal direction. Self-report and one test per domain. Young students at one site. Italian questionnaire version not formally validated. No correction for multiple comparisons is mentioned for the small TV multitasking correlations. It does not show multitasking is harmless or harmful.

## E043: Talking with strangers is informative (Atir, 2022)

- **Type:** RCT (seven experiments in one paper, several preregistered, plus two pretests).
- **Sample:** Varies by experiment, from 103 university participants (Exp 5) to 441 online observers (Exp 6). Includes 206 Chicago park visitors (Exp 1), 198 mostly university students (Exp 2), and 198 at public attractions (Exp 4). Mostly US, majority female in most. Non-clinical. Setting: mixed lab, park, and online.
- **Finding:** People learned more from a 10-minute stranger conversation than they expected (Exp 1 b = 1.14, P < 0.001; Exp 2 b = 1.45, P < 0.001). Null comparisons: no underestimate for web browsing (Exp 4 b = -0.10, P = 0.61) or talking with an acquaintance (Exp 5, Ps > 0.10). Underestimating learning correlated with underestimating enjoyment (r = 0.37) and liking (r = 0.28). A field experiment on London trains, reported by E043, showed no significant underestimate in very short conversations.
- **Limits:** Mainly about learning, not comfort or being liked. Partners had already agreed to talk, so it does not cover approach or rejection. No introversion, shyness, or social anxiety analysis. Learning was mostly self-reported, with some outside-rater checks.
